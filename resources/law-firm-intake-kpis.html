Law Firm Intake KPIs:
What to Track and How to Calculate Them

Track response time, reach, booking, attendance, and hire rates to see where prospective clients stop progressing. Start with consistent definitions, then use the numbers to choose a specific improvement.

By Practice Perfect · Published October 7, 2026

Define the funnel before you build the dashboard.

A useful intake scorecard follows an inquiry from first contact to a hiring decision. Counting calls alone does not tell you whether the firm reached the person, booked a consultation, or retained a client.

Use one record per distinct prospective matter and link repeat calls, forms, and messages to it. Write down which inquiries count as valid. Exclude confirmed spam, vendor solicitations, and duplicates; retain genuine inquiries that the firm declines so you can see the quality of incoming demand. Report exclusions separately rather than quietly removing difficult leads.

Define “reached,” “qualified,” “booked,” “completed consultation,” and “hired” in a shared data dictionary. Qualification should use firm-approved criteria. A hiring event might mean a signed engagement agreement, but your firm must decide the precise milestone and record it consistently.

The definitions below are a recommended reporting framework, not universal CRM defaults. For example, Clio distinguishes lead-to-matter acceptance from matter-to-hired conversion. Check your software’s denominator before comparing its dashboard with your own scorecard.

The intake KPI scorecard.

Show the underlying counts next to every percentage. If a denominator is zero, display “not applicable” rather than 0%. Group rates by the same inquiry cohort and state the outcome cutoff date.

Recommended definitions for a law firm intake scorecard
MetricCalculationWhat it reveals
First response timeFirst human contact attempt time − inquiry received timeHow long a new inquiry waits for a person to act.
Reach rateInquiries with two-way contact ÷ valid inquiries × 100Whether attempts become actual conversations.
Qualification rateQualified inquiries ÷ reached inquiries × 100How much contacted demand fits the firm’s criteria.
Booking rateQualified inquiries with a consultation booked ÷ qualified reached inquiries × 100Whether suitable prospects take the next step.
Consultation show rateCompleted consultations ÷ scheduled consultations due by the cutoff × 100Whether scheduled conversations happen.
Consultation-to-hire rateHired matters following a completed consultation ÷ completed consultations × 100How consultations progress to engagement.
Inquiry-to-hire rateHired matters ÷ valid inquiries × 100The overall outcome of the inquiry cohort.
Channel cost per hireDefined attributable channel cost ÷ hired matters attributed to that channelAcquisition efficiency under a stated cost and attribution model.

Response time: separate attempts from contact.

An automated confirmation is useful, but it is not a human response or proof of two-way contact. Report median response time and the 90th percentile to see both a typical wait and the slower end of the distribution. Also show inquiries with no attempt recorded; excluding them from the time calculation must not make them disappear.

For a live answered call, the response time may be zero. Keep unanswered calls in the inquiry count and record when a person first attempts a callback. State whether elapsed time includes nights and weekends. Business-hours measurements and clock-time measurements answer different questions.

Booking and attendance: count a consistent event.

For the booking rate, count each inquiry once when its initial consultation is booked. For attendance, use initial consultations due by the reporting cutoff, not appointments scheduled for the future. Apply a written reschedule policy: move a rescheduled consultation to its final appointment date and count it once. Show cancellations and no-shows separately alongside attendance so a falling show rate has an explanation.

Hiring and cost: allow outcomes to mature.

A consultation held today may not produce a decision today. Keep pending decisions visible and revisit the cohort at a consistent follow-up interval appropriate to your practice. Do not divide all clients hired this month by this month’s inquiries when some of those clients first contacted you earlier.

Use a documented attribution model, such as recorded first inquiry source, for channel cost reporting. Match the spend period to the inquiry cohort and measure hires from those inquiries through the stated cutoff. Advertising-only cost per hire is not full customer acquisition cost. If you report full acquisition cost, specify the included agency, software, staffing, and other costs. Neither metric establishes profitability or collected revenue.

A worked example: 100 inquiries.

Hypothetical example, not a benchmark.

These invented numbers illustrate the formulas. They are not Practice Perfect client results or targets for your firm.

Suppose one mature inquiry cohort contains 100 valid inquiries. The team reaches 80, qualifies 60, books 45 initial consultations, completes 36, and retains 18 matters after those consultations. All 45 appointments are due by the cutoff; there are no unresolved reschedules, and all hiring decisions are resolved.

  • Reach: 80 ÷ 100 = 80%.
  • Qualification: 60 ÷ 80 = 75%.
  • Booking: 45 ÷ 60 = 75%.
  • Show: 36 ÷ 45 = 80%.
  • Consultation to hire: 18 ÷ 36 = 50%.
  • Inquiry to hire: 18 ÷ 100 = 18%.

If all 100 inquiries belong to one channel cohort with $3,600 in attributable advertising spend, advertising cost per hire is $3,600 ÷ 18 = $200. This excludes other acquisition costs.

The scorecard highlights 20 unreached inquiries and nine consultations that did not occur. Review the underlying records before choosing an intervention. You may find incorrect contact details, missed callbacks, scheduling friction, or a lead-quality issue; the percentages alone do not establish the cause.

Turn the scorecard into a weekly review.

  1. Check the data. Look for duplicates, missing source fields, incomplete outcomes, and inconsistent stage changes before interpreting a trend.
  2. Separate activity from outcomes. Review this week’s calls and appointments for workload planning. Use inquiry cohorts for conversion analysis and label immature cohorts as provisional.
  3. Inspect the open queue. Count inquiries with no assigned owner, no next action, or an overdue follow-up. Group open inquiries by age and confirm who will act.
  4. Compare comparable groups. Break out source and practice area. Display sample sizes and avoid ranking staff whose case mix, hours, or lead sources differ.
  5. Choose one change. Assign an owner, define the action, and set a review date. Examples include callback coverage, clearer booking instructions, or a consistent follow-up task.

Begin with your own baseline rather than an unsupported universal “good” conversion rate. A change in inquiry mix can move the percentages even when execution stays the same. Compare similar cohorts and review the actual conversations before making staffing or marketing decisions.
